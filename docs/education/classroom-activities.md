# Six Lessons From One Archive
### Standalone classroom activities using the StoryMaps archive of Jewish businesses in Berlin, 1900–1945

*Draft v1 — English. A German edition is planned; this version has not yet been translated or adapted for German curriculum standards.*

---

## What this document is

The companion workbook, **The Street You Walk On**, is a three-phase project built around a walk: students research their own street, go to the addresses, and design a present-day continuation of one business. It needs 4–6 lesson hours and a trip out of the building.

Not every class can do that. These six activities each fit **one lesson**, need **no walk**, and can be run in any order or on their own. They use the same archive and are written under the same rules.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Age range** | 13–18 (adaptation notes in each activity) |
| **Group size** | 12–30 |
| **Time per activity** | 45–90 minutes, stated per activity |
| **Setting** | A classroom with at least one screen per pair or group |
| **Core resource** | The StoryMaps map of Jewish businesses in Berlin, 1900–1945 |
| **Prints** | Each activity ends in a worksheet you can print or project |

**Before you run any of these, read the section *Teaching difficult history* in [The Street You Walk On](/education/workbook).** It is not repeated here, and it is not optional. The short version: set the frame before students touch the map, name the actors, never let a business "fail" in your wording, allow students to step back from any part without explaining why, and do not run any of this as a competition.

## Choosing an activity

| # | Activity | Time | Best for | Students end up with |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | One street, one century | 60 min | An entry point; history and geography | A hand-drawn timeline of a single street |
| 2 | Reading the colour | 45 min | Source criticism; media literacy | An honest caption for a misleading colour |
| 3 | What the record does not say | 60 min | Older students; archive ethics | A gap report on five records |
| 4 | Write the plaque | 90 min | Language, ethics, design | A drafted memorial plaque, peer-reviewed |
| 5 | The trade map | 60 min | Economics, geography, maths | A chart of one trade across the city |
| 6 | The order of events | 60 min | Causation; the core history lesson | A matched sequence of decree and consequence |

Activities 1 and 6 are the two to pick if you can only run one. Activity 1 makes the archive personal; activity 6 makes it explicable.

## Shared preparation (about 30 minutes, once)

1. Open the map yourself and move the year slider from 1900 to 1945. Watch what happens between 1933 and 1939.
2. Search one street you know. Note how many records come back and how many have dates. This tells you what your class will actually find.
3. Decide the language your class will use — the site runs in English, German and Yiddish. The plaques and the archival terms are German either way.
4. Print the worksheet for the activity you are running.
5. Have the vocabulary list from the workbook to hand: *Arisierung*, forced sale, liquidation, boycott, trade association, deportation.

**A note on what the archive holds.** About 10,000 records; roughly 2,760 of them carry a trade and dates as well as a name and address; 16 have a full researched narrative with photographs. Most records are directory-level: a name, an address, a trade. Coverage is dense in Mitte, northern Kreuzberg and Charlottenburg, and thin in the outer districts. Tell students this before they search, or they will read a thin result as "nothing happened here".

# Activity 1 — One street, one century

**60 minutes · pairs · History, Geography**

### Aim

Students turn a list of database rows for one street into a timeline they draw themselves, and state in one sentence what happened to that street's Jewish-owned businesses.

### Why this one first

The archive is 10,000 rows. A student cannot feel 10,000 rows. One street with eleven businesses on it, nine of which end between 1936 and 1939, is a thing a fifteen-year-old can hold in their head and repeat to somebody at dinner.

### Preparation

Choose three or four streets in advance and check each returns a workable number of records — six to fifteen is ideal. Good dense candidates: Friedrichstrasse, Kronenstrasse, Ritterstrasse, Kantstrasse, Kurfürstendamm. Assign, don't let pairs hunt.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | **Frame it.** State plainly what students are about to look at: these were shops, workshops and offices; they were taken; the people who took them were often local. Show the map on the projector and move the slider once, slowly, from 1930 to 1940. Say nothing while you do it. |
| 10–15 | Hand out the worksheet. Assign each pair a street. |
| 15–35 | **Collect.** Pairs list every business the archive holds for their street: name, trade, start year, end year. Records with no dates get recorded too, in the "no dates held" box — that is data, not a failure. |
| 35–50 | **Draw.** Each pair draws a horizontal line, 1900 on the left, 1945 on the right, and marks each business as a bar from its start year to its end year. Undated businesses go underneath, as a separate list. |
| 50–60 | **Say it.** Each pair reads out one sentence: *"On ____ street the archive holds ____ businesses. ____ of them end between ____ and ____."* Then one pair explains what the shape of their line looks like. Do not summarise for them; let the sentences accumulate. |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** cap it at six businesses per pair; supply the timeline axis pre-drawn on the worksheet.
- **16–18:** add a second axis — trade. Ask whether businesses in the same trade end at the same time, and what that would mean if true.
- **Fast finishers:** find the earliest founding date on the street and the latest end date, and work out how many years of continuous trading the street loses in total.

### Assessment

Accurate transcription, including the undated records. A correctly proportioned timeline. A closing sentence that names a range of years rather than "the Nazis".

### Pitfalls

- Students will want a "reason" written next to each end date. The archive usually does not hold one. Have them write *not recorded* — and then discuss why so many end dates cluster anyway.
- Do not let the exercise become a race for the street with the most businesses.

# Activity 2 — Reading the colour

**45 minutes · whole class then pairs · Media literacy, History**

### Aim

Students work out what the map's status colours actually encode, identify what the design does not say, and write a caption that would prevent a stranger from misreading it.

### Why

The map shows each business as **open**, **under pressure**, **closed**, or **not yet founded**. "Under pressure" is a real historical state — boycott, exclusion from trade associations, refused licences, blocked accounts, customers driven away — but a colour cannot say that. Left unexplained, a student reads amber as "the business was doing badly", which quietly moves the cause from a state and its citizens onto the victims. Catching that is a transferable skill: it is the same reading students need for any data visualisation.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Project the map at 1936 with no explanation. Ask: *what does each colour mean?* Take answers. Write them up verbatim, including the wrong ones. Do not correct yet. |
| 10–20 | Reveal what the states encode, using the workbook's wording. Ask the class to compare their guesses with the real definitions and mark which guesses blamed the business. |
| 20–35 | **Pairs:** write a 25-word caption for the amber state that a stranger could not misread. Then write a one-sentence description of what the map cannot show at all. |
| 35–45 | Read four captions aloud. Vote on the clearest — not the most dramatic. Close by naming three things the map genuinely cannot show: the owners' names in most cases, who bought the business, and everyone who was never in a directory in the first place. |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** supply three candidate captions, one blaming, one vague, one accurate; students choose and justify.
- **16–18:** ask what a fifth colour would have to encode to make the map more honest, and what data it would need. Then ask whether the map should be redesigned or annotated — and who decides.

### Assessment

A caption that names an actor. Any caption using "struggled", "went under" or "lost the shop" has not met the objective, and the rewrite is the lesson.

# Activity 3 — What the record does not say

**60 minutes · threes · Source criticism (best for 16–18)**

### Aim

Students audit five real records for what is missing, classify each gap, and propose what evidence would close it.

### Why

An archive is not a photograph of the past. It is what survived, of what was written down, of what somebody chose to record. A record with no end date is not a business that did not end. Students who can say that out loud are doing history rather than collecting facts.

### Preparation

Pick five records in advance with a deliberate spread: one of the 16 with a full narrative, two with dates and a trade, two directory-level entries with almost nothing. Do not tell students the spread is deliberate.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Ask: *where do you think this data came from?* Explain: a scholarly database at Humboldt-Universität zu Berlin, built from directories, registers and case files, re-verified against its source. Then ask what kinds of business would never appear in a trade directory at all. |
| 10–35 | Groups audit their five records against the worksheet: which of the four facts are present, what else is held, what is missing. |
| 35–50 | For each gap, groups classify it: **never recorded**, **recorded but lost**, **exists but not yet digitised**, or **unknowable**. Then they name one source that could close it — a *Handelsregister* entry, an address book, a compensation file, a newspaper advertisement, a family. |
| 50–60 | Each group states the one gap they would most want closed, and why that one. |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** run with three records and only two gap categories (*never recorded* / *lost*).
- **16–18:** add the hard question — the database is dense in Mitte and thin in Spandau. How much of that is history and how much is where research has been done? What would you need to tell the difference?

### Assessment

Correct identification of present vs missing facts; a defensible gap classification; a named source that plausibly exists.

### Pitfalls

Students may conclude the archive is "unreliable". Redirect: an archive that states its gaps is more reliable than one that fills them. The failure mode is invention, not absence.

# Activity 4 — Write the plaque

**90 minutes · pairs, then peer review · Language, Ethics, Design**

### Aim

Students draft a memorial plaque for one business under a strict factual rule, review each other's against that rule, and compare their draft with the one this project generates.

### Why

This is the activity that turns research into a decision. Every word on a memorial in a street has to be defended: too vague and a passer-by learns nothing, too dramatic and it stops being about the business, one guessed fact and the whole object is untrustworthy.

### The rule students work under

A plaque may state only four facts, and it must state **all four**:

1. the business name,
2. the trade,
3. the address,
4. the start and end year.

**A record missing any of the four does not get a plaque.** The gap is never filled with a plausible guess. This is the rule the project's own plaque generator enforces in code — of about 10,000 records, 2,464 qualify.

Alongside the four facts, every plaque carries:

- the word **GEDENKTAFEL** (memorial plaque) and **HIER STAND** ("here stood"), so a stranger knows what the object is;
- a standing context line: *„Jüdisches Unternehmen, unter nationalsozialistischer Herrschaft enteignet und liquidiert."* — "A Jewish business, expropriated and liquidated under National Socialist rule";
- the funder's name, **Stiftung Zurückgeben**;
- a QR code back to the full record.

Discuss the context line with the class before they draft. *Enteignet und liquidiert* is what the records document: expropriation under the "Aryanisation" programme, then wind-up — which is where the end dates come from. Ask why a vaguer verb would be dishonest, and why a more dramatic one would be too.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–15 | Show the two real formats — the 300 × 200 mm narrative plaque and the 300 × 100 mm compact one — on the [plaque initiative page](/plaques). Read the compact one out, line by line. Ask what each line is doing. |
| 15–25 | Introduce the four-facts rule. Give pairs three candidate records, one of which is deliberately incomplete. Ask which is not eligible and why. |
| 25–55 | **Draft.** Each pair writes a compact plaque for an eligible record on the worksheet, at real size (a 300 × 100 mm rectangle is roughly A4 landscape cut in half lengthways — hand out the pre-drawn frame). |
| 55–75 | **Peer review.** Pairs swap. Reviewers check against the rule sheet: are all four facts present? Is any fact invented? Does a passer-by learn what this object is? Is the language accurate about who acted? |
| 75–90 | Compare two or three student drafts with the generated plaque for the same record. Where they differ, ask which is better and why — sometimes the students' will be. Close on the last question: *should this go up, and who has to agree?* (The answer includes the building's owner.) |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** supply the fixed lines pre-printed; students complete only the four facts and choose between three offered context lines.
- **16–18:** ask them to write the plaque in German and in English and defend the differences. Then ask what a plaque for a business whose owner's fate is unknown may claim.

### Assessment

Four facts present and correct; nothing invented; the object self-explains; language names actors. A beautiful plaque with a guessed founding date fails.

# Activity 5 — The trade map

**60 minutes · fours · Economics, Geography, Maths**

### Aim

Students take one trade, map where in the city it clustered, and explain the cluster — then say what the exclusion of that trade would have done to the neighbourhood around it.

### Why

Jewish commercial life in Berlin was not evenly spread and not evenly distributed across trades. The garment quarter around Hausvogteiplatz is the clearest case: a dense, interdependent cluster of cutters, wholesalers, trimmings suppliers and fashion houses. Destroying it did not remove businesses one at a time; it removed an industry, and the neighbourhood that lived off it.

### Preparation

The archive sorts businesses into 24 trades. Assign one per group. Good ones: textiles and clothing (by far the largest), food and drink, banking, chemicals and pharmacy, furniture, metal goods, leather and shoes, books and art.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Ask the class to guess the most common trade in the archive, and where it clustered. Record the guesses. |
| 10–35 | Groups filter for their trade and record: how many businesses, which districts, which streets recur, the earliest and latest dates they can find. |
| 35–50 | Each group produces one simple chart — a bar per district, or a tally on an outline map. Hand-drawn is fine and often better. |
| 50–60 | Groups present in one minute: *our trade, our number, our cluster, our explanation.* Then the whole-class question: what happens to a street when the trade it lived on is removed inside five years? |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** supply the district list; students tally only.
- **16–18:** compare two trades and ask why one clusters tightly and the other spreads. Introduce the idea of an interdependent supply chain: who else on that street lost their customers when the wholesaler closed?
- **Maths link:** express each district's share as a percentage; discuss why a raw count misleads when districts differ in size.

### Assessment

Accurate counts, a legible chart, and an explanation of the cluster that names a reason — proximity to suppliers, to customers, to a market hall, to a station — rather than restating the pattern.

# Activity 6 — The order of events

**60 minutes · fours · History (causation)**

### Aim

Students place the measures that destroyed Jewish commercial life in order, match them to what the archive shows happening, and state the relationship between the two in their own words.

### Why

Without this lesson, the map is a mystery: businesses simply stop. With it, the map is evidence. Every darkened point has a decree behind it and a beneficiary in front of it, and students can name both.

### Preparation

Print and cut the cards below — one set per group, shuffled. Keep the dates on the cards; the task is ordering **and** matching, not memorising dates.

### The cards

| Card | Measure |
|---|---|
| A | **1 April 1933** — organised nationwide boycott of Jewish-owned shops, with SA members posted at the doors. |
| B | **1933 onwards** — exclusion from trade associations and professional bodies; loss of public contracts and of standing to bid for them. |
| C | **15 September 1935** — the Nuremberg Laws strip Jewish Germans of citizenship rights, deepening exclusion from public and commercial life. |
| D | **26 April 1938** — Jewish-owned property above 5,000 Reichsmark must be registered with the state; the inventory that makes systematic seizure possible. |
| E | **14 June 1938** — a decree defines and registers "jüdische Gewerbebetriebe", making every such business identifiable on paper. |
| F | **9–10 November 1938** — the November pogrom: shops and synagogues attacked and destroyed across Germany; tens of thousands of men arrested. |
| G | **12 November 1938** — Jews are barred from operating retail businesses and trades from 1 January 1939. |
| H | **3 December 1938** — decree on the "utilisation" of Jewish property: forced sale or liquidation of what remains. |
| I | **From October 1941** — systematic deportations from Berlin begin. |

**Answer key:** A · B · C · D · E · F · G · H · I. Cards B and C overlap in time — accept either order with a justification, and use the disagreement.

### Run

| Time | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0–10 | Groups order the cards face up. No devices yet. |
| 10–20 | Reveal the order. Discuss the pairs students swapped and why the sequence is not obvious. Name the pattern: register, then exclude, then seize. |
| 20–40 | **Match to the archive.** Groups open the map, move the slider through 1933, 1935, 1938, 1939 and 1941, and record what changes at each step. If your class ran Activity 1, use their own street timelines instead. |
| 40–55 | Each group writes the causal sentence for two cards: *"After ____, the archive shows ____, because ____."* |
| 55–60 | One closing question, answered in writing, not aloud: **who benefited?** Every forcibly sold business had a buyer. The word for what happened to the shop is not "closure" — it is transfer. |

### Differentiation

- **13–15:** use six cards (A, C, F, G, H, I) and supply the causal sentence stems.
- **16–18:** add the harder framing — a decree is a permission, not a cause on its own. Boycotts and denunciations were carried out by neighbours and competitors. Ask what conditions make that participation possible, and be ready for the conversation this opens.

### Assessment

Correct sequence; at least one causal sentence that connects a dated measure to a change visible in the archive; an answer to "who benefited" that names a category of person rather than "the Nazis".

### Pitfalls

- Do not end on the deportation card as a dramatic reveal. End on the beneficiary question — that is the part that transfers to the present.
- Watch the correlation trap: businesses that end in 1938 are strong evidence, but the archive's end dates are not all equally documented. Say so.

## Assessment across the six

If you run more than one activity, these criteria apply to all of them and are worth stating to students at the start.

| Criterion | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **Accuracy** | Facts transcribed correctly from the archive; gaps recorded as gaps. |
| **Attribution** | Any claim beyond the record is sourced or marked as inference. |
| **Language** | Active voice; actors named; no wording that attributes the outcome to the victims. |
| **Restraint** | Nothing added for drama; the evidence is allowed to be as bad as it is. |
| **Judgement** | The student can say what they do not know, and what would be needed to find out. |

Do not grade emotional response, and do not require it.

## Adaptations

**Outside Berlin.** Activities 2, 3, 4 and 6 work unchanged anywhere — they are about reading evidence, not about Berlin streets. For 1 and 5, substitute your own city's records if a comparable archive exists locally; if not, run them on a Berlin district and treat the distance as the point of discussion.

**Without devices.** Print 20–30 records in advance as a paper deck. Activities 1, 3, 4 and 6 run entirely on paper. Activity 5 becomes a tally from the deck.

**In one lesson only.** Activity 1 (60 min) or Activity 6 (60 min). Activity 2 fits a short period.

**As a project week.** Run 1 → 6 → 3 → 4 in sequence, then the walk from *The Street You Walk On* as the finish.
